Cross-Functional Collaboration: Experiential Learning Design

A simulation-based half-day experience that helps senior managers practise making one decision from four partial pictures.

The brief

What the organisation asked for, and how I read the problem behind it.

Context

A well-known technology company asked for a half-day experience for 25 senior managers to strengthen how they work across functions.

Challenge

  • Departments were operating in silos.
  • Communication and coordination were poor.
  • Together, these recently contributed to a major project-delivery setback.

Objective

Build trust and enhance cross-functional collaboration among leaders.

How I framed it. Senior leaders often do not lack information. They lack a shared picture of the problem. Each function holds legitimate information, reads it through its own priorities and fills gaps with assumptions. Different perspectives are not the problem. A fragmented picture is.

Design challenge I set myself

How might we help 25 senior managers experience what happens when capable functions decide with a partial picture, and practise the behaviours that turn different perspectives into a shared decision they can own?

Why a simulation instead of presenting concepts

Three commitments shaped the design.

The usual response

Explain a collaboration model

Senior managers have seen collaboration models before. A model delivered first can be nodded at and left in the room.

The design choice

Let them feel it first, then name it

Participants make a real decision with distributed information. The debrief creates the need for the framework before it is shown, so the framework names something they have already felt.

Experience before explanation

Leaders feel what a fragmented picture does to a decision, then name the behaviours themselves.

Differences are material, not a fault

Every function stays legitimate. No function is the problem and none holds the obvious answer.

Practise, then transfer

Two attempts at the same decision, then one real workplace situation to apply the behaviours to.

Why a fictional company. The distance is deliberate. Leaders can examine their own patterns without reopening the real project setback in front of peers. The real work comes back in My Silo Moment, on each participant's own terms.

The learning experience

One arc in eight moves. The framework arrives fifth, after participants have already lived the problem and started to describe the fix.

  1. 1

    Experience

    Reflection and Round 1

    Recall real partial pictures, then decide as four functions with distributed information.

  2. 2

    Notice

    Round 1 debrief: what happened?

    Reconstruct what they knew, what they lacked and when things changed.

  3. 3

    Connect

    Debrief: how did we work? What was the impact?

    Link their own behaviour to the quality and speed of the decision.

  4. 4

    Name

    Replay

    Say, in their own words, what they would do differently.

  5. 5

    Frame

    SHARE, SEEK, SURFACE, ALIGN

    See their own behaviours named, then practise them against Round 1.

  6. 6

    Re-practise

    Round 2 and debrief

    Use the behaviours deliberately when conditions change.

  7. 7

    Transfer

    My Silo Moment and Peer Exchange

    Apply the lens to a live workplace situation with a peer.

  8. 8

    Commit

    Commitment Card

    Choose one specific, observable behaviour to try next.

The shape of the 3 hours 45 minutes

  • Set up0:00 to 0:30. Welcome, reflection in mixed trios, simulation briefing.
  • Experience0:30 to 1:20. Function preparation, Round 1, Round 1 debrief.
  • Break1:20 to 1:30.
  • Name and frame1:30 to 2:05. Framework, then practice on Round 1.
  • Re-practise2:05 to 2:45. Round 2 with a new event, then debrief.
  • Transfer and commit2:45 to 3:45. My Silo Moment, Peer Exchange, commitment, close.

Inside the simulation: The Launch Decision

A fictional company, NexaTech, is six weeks from launching NexaFlow. The project is officially on track, but different functions see different readiness signals. Everyone gets the same common brief. Each function then receives only its own brief.

ProductProtects customer value and the credibility of the proposition.
TechnologyProtects reliability and technical readiness.
CommercialProtects customer confidence, market momentum and opportunity.
OperationsProtects implementation quality, support capacity and readiness.

One collective recommendation from four options, with no predetermined correct answer:

Launch as planned
Phase the launch
Reduce initial scope
Delay the launch

The group must also explain what it is protecting, what it is willing to compromise, the biggest risk it is accepting, and what needs to happen next.

Round 1

Twenty minutes, one decision room, distributed information. The facilitator observes the system and does not steer.

Round 2

A new event card changes the conditions: an important customer asks for an earlier date. Groups revisit the decision, using SHARE, SEEK, SURFACE and ALIGN deliberately.

The behavioural framework

Four observable behaviours, introduced from the participants' own replay answers rather than from a slide.

SHARE

Make relevant information visible early.

What does another function need to know to make a better decision?
SEEK

Understand before assuming or proposing.

What might I be missing?
SURFACE

Make risks, constraints and disagreements discussable.

What needs to be made visible before we decide?
ALIGN

Turn different perspectives into a shared decision.

What are we collectively trying to achieve, and what trade-offs are we willing to own?

Alignment is not agreement. It requires enough shared understanding to make and own a decision together.

Design decisions worth noting

Purposeful gamification

Engagement comes from the mechanics: distributed information, time pressure, trade-offs, changing conditions and re-practice. There are no points, badges, leaderboards or competition between groups.

Do not rescue

Productive friction is part of the learning. The guide tells facilitators to wait, then ask a question, and never supply the missing information.

No pre-set structure

No chair, spokespeople or round-robin is suggested. How the room organises itself is part of what gets debriefed.

Honest about Round 2

The guide plans for Round 2 going worse and treats that as useful: "What made the behaviours harder under pressure?"

What this page claims, and what it does not

This is a design sample. It describes what was designed and intended.

Designed and documented

  • The experience creates opportunities to practise behaviours that can strengthen working trust.
  • The guide plans how to observe Round 1 against Round 2 using observation sheets.
  • It plans end-of-session indicators: spotting a real silo, naming the missing behaviour, and how specific the commitment is.
  • It offers an optional 30-day check-in with three questions: which behaviour did you try, what happened, what got in the way.

Not claimed here

  • Participant feedback or evaluation scores.
  • Business outcomes or return on investment.
  • Trust scores or measured behaviour change.

The guide itself says that an afternoon does not build trust, and that results should be reported as observed, in plain terms.
